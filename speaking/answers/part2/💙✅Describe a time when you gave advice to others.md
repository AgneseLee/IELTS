I gave advice to Stella, my former colleague, after our company went through layoffs last year. Her <mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">workload</mark> had become much heavier, and she was sleeping badly and trying to solve every problem alone. I first listened to her describe the pressure. Then I suggested <mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">separating</mark> urgent tasks from optional ones, discussing impossible deadlines with her manager, and protecting a basic routine of sleep and exercise. I gave this advice because I had recently faced a <mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">layoff</mark> myself and<mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;"> understood the fear</mark> behind her stress. <!-- REFLECTION_START -->
This experience taught me that useful advice should not sound like a <mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">lecture</mark>. <mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">Communication</mark> matters because people need to feel understood and supported before they can consider a different approach.<mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;"> Problem-solving </mark>matters as well, but the steps must be realistic for that person's situation.

Looking back, I became more <mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">empathetic</mark> when giving advice. I now listen first, ask what the person can control, and offer only a few manageable <mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">steps</mark>. Good advice won't help if someone feels judged or pushed. I try to support people <mark style="background: #ABF7F7A6;">without deciding</mark> for them.
