## Cue Card

**Describe a time you needed to use your imagination**

> **You should say:**
> - When it was
> - Why you needed to use imagination
> - How difficult or easy it was
> - And explain how you felt about it

> Bank: B4 | Modules: vague-request / prototype / user-feedback

## 1. Core Ability Mapping

- Primary Ability: Creativity & Innovation
- Reason: I imagined several user journeys and turned a vague request into a testable software prototype.
- Secondary Ability: Problem-solving Skills
- Reason: I used feedback to remove unnecessary steps and find a practical solution within tight limits.

## 2. Story Bank

- Who/What: I designed a software prototype for a client.
- Background: The request was vague, with no existing example and little time.
- Main event: I sketched alternatives, built a simple version, and tested it with users.
- Ability shown: Practical creativity supported by iterative problem-solving.
- Reflection: Imagination became a method for exploring and testing possible solutions.

## 3. Band 7 Answer (1:40–2:00)

<!-- NARRATIVE_START -->
I needed to use my imagination during a freelance software project last year. A client wanted a visual board for tracking work, but the request was vague and there was no existing example to copy. I had to imagine how a first-time user would move through the system. I sketched several screen layouts and built a simple prototype rather than a polished product. It was fairly difficult because I had limited time and technical constraints. The first version was too complicated, but user feedback helped me remove unnecessary steps. Eventually, the client approved a much clearer design.
<!-- NARRATIVE_END -->

<!-- REFLECTION_START -->
I felt satisfied because imagination solved a real problem. One user completed the process without instructions, showing that the idea worked.

The project strengthened my creativity by teaching me to explore possibilities before choosing an answer. It also improved my problem-solving because every idea had to survive testing and practical limits. I kept the failed sketches because they explained later choices. Looking back, professional imagination means picturing possible futures, observing responses, and turning the best one into reality. I now have a reusable method for vague requests and feel much less anxious when clients cannot clearly describe exactly what they truly need at first in practice.
<!-- REFLECTION_END -->
