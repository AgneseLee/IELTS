## 1. Tall building you like/dislike

### Keywords and collocations

office skyscraper · Beijing · glass exterior · natural light · city view · efficient land use · crowded lifts · mixed feelings

### Complete answer

The tall building I'd like to describe is an office skyscraper near my former workplace in Beijing. Its glass exterior reflects the sky, and the entrance opens into a bright atrium filled with natural light. From the upper floors, I could see **the city stretching in every direction**, which was impressive at sunset. I liked the building because it used limited urban land efficiently and placed offices, cafés and transport links close together. However, the lifts became crowded during rush hour, and the sealed windows sometimes made the interior feel disconnected from nature. Overall, I like its clean design and convenience, but I would not want every building to look the same. A successful tall building should combine visual impact with **a comfortable experience for the people inside**.

### Part 3

**Q: Are there many tall buildings in China?** A: Yes, especially in large cities where land is expensive and development is **dense and fast**.

**Q: Why do tall buildings differ?** A: Function, budget, climate, regulations and local identity produce **different structural and visual choices**.

**Q: Why are places designed differently?** A: Geography, population, history and economic needs shape how land and transport are **organised over time**.

**Q: What are the advantages of living high up?** A: Views, light, security and efficient land use, balanced against lifts, evacuation and **weaker street-level connection**.

**Q: Why remodel homes themselves?** A: To save money, express identity and gain satisfaction from **direct personal involvement**.

**Q: What interior styles are popular?** A: Simple modern interiors with neutral colours and practical storage because urban homes often have **limited space**.

## 2. Boring place

### Keywords and collocations

airport waiting area · delayed flight · artificial lighting · crowded seats · repetitive announcements · little to do · trapped feeling · purpose matters

### Complete answer

The boring place was an airport waiting area where Stella and I spent almost six hours after a flight delay. The space had rows of hard seats, bright artificial lighting and no windows near our gate. I could hear **the same announcements repeated continuously**, and every café was overcrowded. We had already checked in, so we could not explore the city or do anything meaningful. We talked, read and walked around, but the uncertainty made time move slowly. I found it boring not because airports are always uninteresting, but because we had no control over the situation and no clear departure time. The experience showed me that boredom often comes from **waiting without purpose or choice**, rather than from the physical place alone.

### Part 3

**Q: Why do children find education boring?** A: Passive repetition feels disconnected from life. Choice, challenge and practical examples can **make learning personally relevant**.

**Q: Why ignore older people's experience?** A: Young people may think circumstances have changed or dislike unsolicited advice. Conversation works better with **mutual respect**.

**Q: Why do young people feel bored when talking with older people?** A: Their interests and communication styles may differ, but asking about specific experiences can reveal **unexpected common ground**.

**Q: What can people do when bored?** A: Change activity, move physically or set a small goal to **restore attention and purpose**.

**Q: Why are some classes boring, and what helps?** A: One-way delivery and unclear relevance reduce attention; interaction, examples and varied tasks **increase engagement**.

**Q: Why are celebrity stories boring to some people?** A: They may seem repetitive and unrelated to real concerns, offering **little practical or intellectual value**.

## 3. Favourite visited city

### Keywords and collocations

Kuala Lumpur · Malaysia trip · multicultural food · modern skyline · traditional streets · efficient transport · lively atmosphere · accessible variety

### Complete answer

My favourite city that I have visited is Kuala Lumpur. I went there with Stella during our Malaysia trip and first knew it through travel videos. What impressed me was the contrast between **a modern skyline and lively traditional neighbourhoods**. We could hear several languages in the markets and smell food from different cultures within one street. Public transport made the main areas easy to reach, yet some quieter districts still felt relaxed. We visited a museum, tried local dishes and walked around the city in the evening. Compared with cities that offer only one dominant experience, Kuala Lumpur felt varied without being impossible to navigate. It is my favourite because it combined cultural diversity, convenient transport and **a welcoming atmosphere for first-time visitors**.

### Part 3

**Q: Is urban or rural life better for different ages?** A: Cities offer education and jobs for young people; quieter areas may suit older people, although preferences depend on **health and lifestyle**.

**Q: How do people choose travel cities?** A: Cost, safety, attractions, food, transport and recommendations shape whether a destination **matches their interests**.

**Q: Is a tourist city good to live in?** A: Sometimes, but crowds, seasonal prices and unstable work can reduce **everyday quality of life**.

**Q: Modern or historical city?** A: Modern cities offer convenience; historical ones offer atmosphere and cultural depth. Many travellers prefer **a mixture of both**.

**Q: What facilities do big cities have?** A: Specialised hospitals, universities, transport and cultural venues are viable because large populations create **sufficient shared demand**.

**Q: Are modern cities better for young or old people?** A: They often suit young workers, but accessible transport and healthcare can also support older residents if design is **inclusive rather than speed-focused**.

**Q: What factors matter before visiting a city?** A: Safety, cost, weather, transport and whether attractions match **the traveller's actual interests**.

**Q: What are the disadvantages of a famous city?** A: High prices, crowds and commercialised attractions can reduce **authentic local life and visitor comfort**.

**Q: Do you prefer developed or historical cities?** A: Historical cities, provided transport is practical, because they offer **a stronger sense of place and continuity**.

**Q: Do city residents live there by choice or necessity?** A: Usually both: jobs and services pull people in, while family and housing costs may **limit their realistic alternatives**.

## 4. Place you recommend

### Keywords and collocations

Semporna · Sabah · clear water · island boats · sea turtles · local seafood · protect environment · memorable escape

### Complete answer

The place I would recommend is Semporna in Sabah, Malaysia. I visited it with Stella last May. It is known for clear water, small islands and rich marine life. What impressed me first was **the extraordinary colour and clarity of the sea**. We took a boat between islands, tried local seafood and went freediving, where I saw a sea turtle moving quietly below us. The town itself is simple, but the surrounding natural environment is unforgettable. I would recommend it to people who enjoy the sea and do not need luxury shopping or nightlife. Visitors should choose responsible operators and avoid damaging coral or leaving rubbish. The place gave me a genuine break from city pressure and **deepened my appreciation for marine conservation**.

### Part 3

**Q: Where do Chinese people go on holiday?** A: Coastal cities, scenic areas and culturally famous cities remain popular because they offer **convenient, varied experiences**.

**Q: Where do young people go?** A: Places with outdoor activities, distinctive food and shareable scenery, often chosen through **online recommendations**.

**Q: What is the ideal holiday length?** A: About one week for many trips: enough to slow down without creating **excessive cost or work disruption**.

**Q: How do people plan holidays?** A: They compare transport, accommodation, weather and reviews, then balance fixed bookings with **some flexible time**.

**Q: Is advance planning important?** A: Yes for cost and availability, though an overfilled itinerary can **remove spontaneity and rest**.

## 5. Home you visit but would not live in

### Keywords and collocations

Stella's studio · city centre · stylish interior · large windows · convenient location · traffic noise · limited storage · visit-versus-live distinction

### Complete answer

The home belongs to Stella and is a small studio in central Beijing. I enjoy visiting because it has large windows, warm lighting and several of her watercolour paintings. The space feels **creative, personal and extremely welcoming**. Its location is also convenient for cafés and museums, so we often meet there before going out. However, I would not live there because the traffic noise continues late at night, the kitchen is tiny and there is almost no storage. Since I work from home frequently, I also need a separate desk area. The flat is perfect for short visits and conversation, but daily comfort depends on practical details that visitors may not notice. It taught me that an attractive home is not automatically **well suited to every person's routine**.

### Part 3

**Q: Do Chinese people visit others' homes?** A: Yes, especially close friends and relatives, although urban socialising increasingly happens in **restaurants and cafés**.

**Q: What do visitors do?** A: Share food, drink tea, talk and sometimes bring a small gift to **show appreciation to the host**.

**Q: What homes do people prefer?** A: Safe, comfortable homes near transport and services, with enough privacy and **affordable long-term costs**.

**Q: How do city and country homes differ?** A: City homes are smaller and more connected; rural homes offer space and quiet but **fewer nearby services**.

## 6. Interesting building

### Keywords and collocations

art museum · Beijing · curved white walls · spacious interior · natural light · guide movement · temporary exhibitions · calm reflection

### Complete answer

The interesting building is an art museum near my apartment in Beijing. Its exterior has curved white walls rather than a conventional rectangular shape. Inside, skylights allow natural light to move across the surfaces, creating **a calm and constantly changing atmosphere**. The corridors gently guide visitors between galleries without making the route feel forced. I first visited with Stella for a watercolour exhibition and noticed that the building itself encouraged us to slow down. It functions as both an exhibition space and a community venue for talks and workshops. I find it interesting because the architecture supports its purpose rather than merely seeking attention. The museum shows how thoughtful design can **influence people's mood and behaviour** while remaining practical.

### Part 3

**Q: What buildings are popular in China?** A: Mixed-use towers and modern cultural venues are common in cities, alongside growing interest in **preserving historic buildings**.

**Q: Is expensive exterior design worthwhile?** A: Only when it improves durability, identity or public space; decoration without function can **waste resources**.

**Q: Is inside or outside more important?** A: The interior affects daily users more, while the exterior shapes public streets. Good design should **balance both responsibilities**.

**Q: Why visit historical sites?** A: They make history tangible and allow people to understand **how earlier communities lived**.

**Q: Should interesting buildings charge entry?** A: Reasonable fees support maintenance, but discounts and free periods preserve **broad public access**.

**Q: Is a new or old building better to live in?** A: New buildings offer efficiency; old ones offer character. Safety, maintenance and location matter **more than age alone**.

## 7. Interesting/famous city

### Keywords and collocations

Zhanjiang · southern China · coastline · seafood · coconut trees · slower pace · developing transport · personal connection

### Complete answer

The city is Zhanjiang, my hometown in southern China. It is famous for its coastline, fresh seafood and tropical character. Streets near the sea are lined with coconut trees, and the air often carries **a light salty smell from the coast**. I knew the city through everyday childhood life rather than tourism, so ordinary places hold personal meaning. Compared with Beijing, it has a slower pace and less pressure, although career options are more limited. Recent transport development has made it easier to reach, while the local food and scenery remain distinctive. I find it interesting because it combines natural beauty with memories of family and childhood. It reminds me that a city can matter not through global fame but through **a deep sense of belonging**.

### Part 3

**Q: What advantages does tourism bring?** A: Visitor spending supports jobs, tax revenue and preservation, provided benefits **reach local communities**.

**Q: Why do young people like cities?** A: Cities concentrate education, employment, entertainment and social networks, creating **more possible life paths**.

**Q: Where do older people live?** A: Many remain in cities for healthcare and family, while others choose rural areas for **space and lower pressure**.

**Q: Can tourism harm locals?** A: Yes, through crowding, rising rents and cultural commercialisation unless development is **carefully managed**.

**Q: What are urbanisation's benefits?** A: Efficient services, larger labour markets and infrastructure can improve **access and productivity**.

**Q: Will big Chinese cities grow larger?** A: Some will expand regionally, but planning may shift growth to connected satellite cities to **reduce central congestion**.

## 8. Place to relax

### Keywords and collocations

neighbourhood park · weekly visit · pond · birds and leaves · fresh smell · evening walk · clear head · accessible refuge

### Complete answer

The place where I relax is a park near my Beijing apartment. I visit it roughly once a week, usually in the evening or at weekends. What impressed me first was a pond surrounded by flowers and tall trees. I can hear birds and leaves rather than constant traffic, and after rain the air has **a fresh, earthy smell**. I walk slowly, listen to music or sit on a bench and read. Sometimes Stella joins me, and we have long conversations without interruptions. Compared with commercial entertainment, the park is free, nearby and requires no planning. It matters because it gives me **a simple escape from screens and work pressure** and usually leaves me feeling calm and refreshed.

### Part 3

**Q: Why is relaxation difficult today?** A: Constant messages, long working hours and blurred boundaries keep people's attention **permanently activated**.

**Q: What are exercise's benefits?** A: It improves physical health, sleep and mood and provides **a constructive release for stress**.

## 9. Natural place

### Keywords and collocations

Semporna islands · Malaysia · turquoise water · coral · quiet waves · freediving · sea turtle · fragile ecosystem

### Complete answer

The natural place I'd like to describe is the island area near Semporna in Malaysia. I visited with Stella last May. The water was so clear that we could see coral and fish from the boat, while the only constant sound was **the gentle movement of waves**. During a freediving trip, a sea turtle passed below us, moving slowly through the turquoise water. Compared with crowded urban attractions, the area felt open and peaceful. I spent most of the day swimming, observing marine life and taking only a few photographs. The place matters to me because it gave me a direct sense of how beautiful yet fragile marine ecosystems are. I returned feeling refreshed and **more personally responsible for protecting the natural world**.

### Part 3

**Q: Why do people visit natural places?** A: To escape noise, exercise and experience environments that offer **perspective beyond everyday routines**.

**Q: How can natural places be protected?** A: Limit visitor pressure, enforce waste rules, fund conservation and ensure tourism operators **benefit the ecosystem and local community**.
