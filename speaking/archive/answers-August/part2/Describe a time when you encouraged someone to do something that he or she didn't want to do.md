## Cue Card

**Describe a time when you encouraged someone to do something that he/she didn't want to do**

> **You should say:**
> - Who he or she is
> - What you encouraged him/her to do
> - How he/she reacted
> - And explain why you encouraged him/her to do it

> Bank: B4 | Modules: burnout / short-walk / gradual-change

## 1. Core Ability Mapping

- Primary Ability: Communication Skills
- Reason: I listened to Stella's concerns and presented exercise as a small, pressure-free choice.
- Secondary Ability: Leadership & Collaboration
- Reason: I supported and motivated her by joining the walk instead of simply telling her what to do.

## 2. Story Bank

- Who/What: I encouraged Stella to take a short walk during burnout.
- Background: Company layoffs had increased her workload and reduced her energy.
- Main event: I offered to walk with her for only fifteen minutes, and she reluctantly agreed.
- Ability shown: Empathetic communication and practical encouragement.
- Reflection: Respecting her choice helped the activity become a lasting habit.

## 3. Band 7 Answer (1:40–2:00)

<!-- NARRATIVE_START -->
I encouraged my close friend Stella to take a short walk when she was burned out after company layoffs. She was exhausted and did not want another demanding activity, so I avoided suggesting a gym programme. Instead, I invited her on a fifteen-minute walk and promised we could stop whenever she wanted. She agreed reluctantly, mainly because I offered to go with her. We talked rather than treating it as exercise, and she noticed that she slept better that night. A few weeks later, she chose a longer park route herself.
<!-- NARRATIVE_END -->

<!-- REFLECTION_START -->
I encouraged her because stress had reduced both her energy and confidence, and a small action seemed more realistic than dramatic advice. The experience improved my communication skills because I had to understand her resistance before explaining the idea. It also taught me how to motivate people without taking away their choice.

What truly matters is that I reduced the first barrier and supported her in person. I never turned the walks into a competition. Gradually, they became her own habit. Looking back, encouragement works best when it feels like support rather than pressure. I now listen first, suggest one manageable step, and let the other person decide whether it suits them.
<!-- REFLECTION_END -->
