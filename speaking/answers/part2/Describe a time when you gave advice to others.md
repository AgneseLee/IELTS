## 3. Band 7 Answer (1:40–2:00)

<!-- NARRATIVE_START -->
I gave advice to Stella, my former colleague, after our company went through layoffs last year. Her workload had become much heavier, and she was sleeping badly and trying to solve every problem alone. I first listened to her describe the pressure. Then I suggested separating urgent tasks from optional ones, discussing impossible deadlines with her manager, and protecting a basic routine of sleep and exercise. I gave this advice because I had recently faced a layoff myself and understood the fear behind her stress. She gradually became calmer and set clearer boundaries.
<!-- NARRATIVE_END -->

<!-- REFLECTION_START -->
This experience taught me that useful advice should not sound like a lecture. Communication matters because people need to feel understood and supported before they can consider a different approach. Problem-solving matters as well, but the steps must be realistic for that person's situation.

I checked in occasionally and respected Stella's final decisions. Over time, she challenged unrealistic deadlines and stopped answering non-urgent messages at night. Looking back, I became more empathetic when giving advice. I now listen first, ask what the person can control, and offer only a few manageable steps. Even sensible advice has little value if it ignores someone's emotions or freedom to choose. That lesson improved how I support others.
<!-- REFLECTION_END -->
